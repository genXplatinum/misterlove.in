# Foreword

Colonial rule changed Punjab. It did not create a blank population on which officials wrote every identity. Reform changed Sikh institutions. It did not mean that reformers had no tradition to argue about. Partition inflicted catastrophic violence and displacement. It did not make every member of a religious community an interchangeable perpetrator.

The difficult work is to keep several causes visible at once. Government categories, recruitment, property law, printing, education and community organisation affected one another. People responded with their own religious and political arguments. Those arguments were not always the same, even among people later grouped under one movement's name.

This part is about continuity and change without a master conspiracy. A claim can recognise the effect of colonial administration while refusing to describe Indians as passive objects. It can recognise institutional reform without pretending that the earlier world contained no distinct Sikh identity. It can recognise victims of Partition without borrowing their suffering to justify a new act of exclusion.

# Claim 01: The British invented Sikh identity from nothing

There was no distinct Sikh tradition before British rule, and every later expression of Sikh identity was manufactured by colonial officials.

## 1. Where It Came From

Colonial classification is real and consequential. The claim turns its consequence into total creation. Once this shortcut is accepted, earlier scripture, congregations, initiation, persecution and political formations can all be treated as irrelevant. The later government's influence becomes an explanation that absorbs the earlier history it was supposed to help us understand.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The Guru Granth Sahib, early associated writings and evidence about the Gurus' communities precede British annexation. Sri Gur Sobha supplies an early account of the Khalsa. These are not products of a nineteenth-century Punjab census. Their existence does not prove that every earlier boundary looked like a modern administrative category. [SAINAPATI] [MANN]

## 3. The Real History

British government changed the incentives and categories through which communities were counted, recruited and represented. Sikh reformers also debated identity on religious and institutional grounds. Rajbir Singh Judge's research on reform and sovereignty argues against reducing those debates to a simple choice between colonial construction and indigenous inheritance. [REFORM]

## 4. The Debunk

The creation-from-nothing claim is false. The untouched-tradition claim is also inadequate. Earlier distinctiveness and later institutional change can both be true. A better explanation specifies what changed: definitions, privileges, representation, practice, education or political organisation. One grand label should not erase all those separate processes.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Begin before colonial rule and follow the evidence forward. Describe Sikh agency and government power together. Religious traditions are not frozen objects; recognising their history does not make them fake. The question is how a particular identity was expressed and organised in a particular period, not whether change disqualifies it from existing.

# Claim 02: The Singh Sabha was one organisation with one perfectly uniform intention

Every Singh Sabha participant held the same position, followed one plan and wanted exactly the same form of Sikh identity.

## 1. Where It Came From

A movement's familiar name hides its internal disagreements. Later outcomes are projected backward onto every participant. Once the winning position is treated as the only position that ever existed, reform looks either inevitable or secretly coordinated. People who argued, changed their minds or pursued different priorities disappear.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Reformers argued about scripture, practice and the boundaries of the community. Invoking the same Guru Granth Sahib did not guarantee identical conclusions about every institution. A religious source can provide a shared frame for disagreement. It cannot supply a membership list proving that every nineteenth-century reformer interpreted it in the same way.

## 3. The Real History

Singh Sabha activity developed through different centres and currents, including the commonly distinguished Amritsar and Lahore settings. Education, printing, debate and the memory of sovereignty were significant. Judge's study uses disputes involving Duleep Singh to show an argumentative and contested process, rather than one smooth institutional mind. [REFORM]

## 4. The Debunk

Complete uniformity is an oversimplification. That does not mean the movement had no shared concerns or lasting effects. It means those effects must be traced through actual people and arguments. A later institution's official narrative is one account of the movement, not proof that every earlier participant anticipated the institution in its present form.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Name the organisation, place, period and position. Distinguish the movement's broad concerns from a particular faction's programme. A reader should understand why reform could be both internally contested and historically powerful. Disagreement is part of the process being studied, not an embarrassing interruption that must be deleted from it.

# Claim 03: Earlier overlap proves Sikhs had no real distinction

Because some Punjabi households participated in more than one religious setting, Sikh identity was unreal until a government required exclusive boxes.

## 1. Where It Came From

The claim assumes that an identity is real only if every member obeys a perfectly exclusive boundary. That standard would make many historical communities disappear. Visiting another shrine, sharing vocabulary or living in a mixed family is treated as proof that no meaningful distinction existed at all.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The scripture's inclusion of contributors from varied backgrounds is an actual canonical fact. It does not establish that the Sikh canon, Muslim law, Hindu devotional traditions and every Punjabi practice were interchangeable. Inclusion involves selection and interpretation. A shared word or revered figure is not proof of identical theology. [SGGS1349] [MANN-NANAK]

## 3. The Real History

Boundaries can vary by practice, institution and situation. A person may be identified one way in a census and another way in a local devotional relationship. Historical analysis should explain those uses, including conflicts over boundaries. It need not deny earlier distinction in order to recognise that later administration sharpened some categories.

## 4. The Debunk

The inference fails. Overlap is not nonexistence. Equally, citing a distinct tradition does not prove that every historical household lived by present institutional definitions. The evidence supports asking where distinctions mattered and how they were enforced, rather than choosing between an entirely undifferentiated past and an entirely segregated one.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Let a community have a history of changing boundaries. Explain a shared practice without claiming all beliefs were identical. Explain a conflict without claiming every relationship was hostile. The past becomes easier to understand when categories help describe the evidence rather than decide in advance which evidence may be allowed.

# Claim 04: Every Udasi or Mahant was a fraudulent outsider

Every earlier custodian associated with an Udasi tradition or called a mahant had no legitimate connection to Sikh institutions and committed the same abuses.

## 1. Where It Came From

A reform movement's justified complaints are enlarged into a judgement on every custodian and every earlier institution. The label becomes a substitute for an investigation. Someone can then condemn a person without checking what they managed, how they acquired the role or what they actually did.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Normative disagreement about practice is real. A reformer can argue that a custodian's conduct contradicts Gurmat without establishing that everyone sharing the custodian's label behaved identically. The Sikh Gurdwaras Act provides a statutory framework involving particular institutions, claims and committees; it is not a moral finding about every historical member of a religious current. [SGPC-ACT]

## 3. The Real History

Shrines had different custodial arrangements, endowments and histories. Gurdwara reform arose amid disputes over control, income, practice and accountability. Particular abuses and violent events demand particular evidence. A history of custodianship should distinguish a religious lineage, a management office and a misconduct allegation rather than treating them as the same category.

## 4. The Debunk

The all-frauds claim is unwarranted. It does not follow that reformers' complaints were invented or that existing management needed no change. The right correction protects the specificity of the complaint. A serious accusation becomes stronger when its evidence is named, rather than when it is attached to everyone within reach of a label.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Describe each institution and conflict in its period. Assess the conduct and the basis of authority separately. A reader can recognise the achievements of gurdwara reform while also understanding how earlier custodial arrangements developed. There is no need to make every predecessor identical in order to explain why reform mattered.

# Claim 05: The SGPC has always governed every Sikh institution worldwide

The present SGPC is an unchanged organisation from the Gurus' time, with identical legal authority over every gurdwara and every Sikh everywhere.

## 1. Where It Came From

A prominent institution is mistaken for the whole community across all periods. Its influence is real, so people assume its jurisdiction must be unlimited. Political representation, religious authority, management responsibility and legal powers are then described as though they were one thing and had one origin.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The current conduct code and the Sikh Gurdwaras Act are identifiable sources with their own histories. Neither is a deed signed in the seventeenth century assigning every future overseas gurdwara to one modern organisation. The code's religious significance must be distinguished from the Act's specified legal framework and from institutions' actual adoption of its rules. [SRM] [SGPC-ACT]

## 3. The Real History

The SGPC emerged through the gurdwara reform movement and acquired a statutory framework in 1925. Other gurdwaras and institutions have different management and legal arrangements. Those arrangements should be checked locally; changing legislation and institutional disputes cannot be settled by a universal claim based solely on the SGPC's prominence.

## 4. The Debunk

The unchanged-global-jurisdiction claim is false. Rejecting it does not deny the SGPC's major historical and religious influence. It asks us to distinguish influence from ownership and management from universal representation. A body can matter deeply without every Sikh's position being reducible to its most recent announcement.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Name the institution and the kind of authority concerned. For a legal or management question, consult the applicable current framework. For a religious disagreement, describe which bodies and communities accept which position. Avoid saying all Sikhs decided something when the evidence shows that one significant institution decided it.

# Claim 06: Nonviolent gurdwara reform was passive and politically unimportant

Because Akali volunteers sometimes refused to retaliate when attacked, the reform movement achieved nothing serious and lacked courage.

## 1. Where It Came From

The argument measures courage only through an immediate capacity to strike back. Organised nonretaliation looks like doing nothing if the organisation, risk and objective are removed from view. It also ignores the strategy of making coercion visible and keeping a movement's demands before a wider public.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Ethical discipline cannot be reduced to a permanent instruction either always to fight or never to resist. The justified means and the actual circumstances matter. A community's religious commitment can sustain restraint as well as armed defence. The existence of a martial tradition does not prove that every useful campaign must be violent.

## 3. The Real History

The reform movement included major confrontations such as Nankana Sahib, Guru ka Bagh and Jaito. The 1925 Act supplied an institutional outcome. The history of volunteers, arrests, violence and negotiations belongs in assessing that outcome. The Act alone does not establish every participant's experience, but it defeats the assertion that no significant institutional change followed. [SGPC-ACT]

## 4. The Debunk

Nonretaliation is not proof of passivity, and the no-achievement claim is inaccurate. Nor should a success story erase disagreement, suffering or later political costs. The relevant questions are what the movement demanded, how it pursued the demand and what changed. The number of blows returned is not a sufficient historical measure.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Recognise disciplined action and the people exposed to harm. Explain what was gained and what remained contested. A community's history is not weakened by admitting that different circumstances called for different means. Courage can include the capacity to bear a danger without letting the opponent dictate the movement's whole method.

# Claim 07: Partition guilt belongs to one entire religious community

Every member of one community caused Partition violence, while every member of another community was only a victim or rescuer.

## 1. Where It Came From

Family memory understandably begins with a family's suffering. Propaganda turns that situated memory into an exclusive history. Victims from another community vanish, and perpetrators from one's own are explained away. A painful inheritance is then made into a reason to distrust a person born decades after the events.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Kanara, ang 1299, supplies an ethical challenge to treating another human being as a permanent enemy. That does not deny the need to investigate an attack. It refuses the substitution of a religious label for evidence about a perpetrator. Remembrance and collective blame are different responses to suffering. [SGGS1299]

## 3. The Real History

Punjab's violence involved attacks on Muslim, Sikh and Hindu populations, displacement, organised groups and failures of protection. Records and oral histories differ in location, timing and perspective. The National Archives' selected documents show the need to examine who produced a report and how information was obtained; they cannot stand for every affected household. [PARTITION-ARCHIVE]

## 4. The Debunk

The universal guilt-and-innocence division is false. It does not follow that every actor was equally responsible for every episode. Establish responsibility specifically, including organisation, official decisions and individual acts. The goal is accuracy about unequal responsibility, not a comforting slogan that either everyone or only one community was guilty.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Let each family's suffering be heard without requiring denial of another family's suffering. Name acts and perpetrators where evidence permits. Protect the distinction between historical accountability and hostility toward descendants. The memory of a destroyed home should help explain the harm of collective violence, not prepare the moral language for its repetition.

# Claim 08: One exact Partition death total is beyond dispute

There is a complete register of everyone who died or was displaced during Partition, so any uncertainty about totals is dishonest.

## 1. Where It Came From

A rounded estimate is repeated as an exact count. Different geographical definitions and time periods are omitted. Death, disappearance, abduction and displacement can then be mixed in the same sentence. The result looks precise because a large number appears, although the method behind it has disappeared.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Religious and family remembrance preserve meaning and experience. They do not create a complete administrative census of victims. A scriptural commitment to truth gives no reason to convert a historical estimate into a known exact total. A count must be supported by the records and method that produced it.

## 3. The Real History

Violence and movement disrupted documentation. Different studies use different periods and geographical scopes; estimates should be identified accordingly. A report from one district or one month is a valuable source with a limited coverage. Oral testimony can restore a life to the account without supplying a total for the whole subcontinent. [PARTITION-ARCHIVE]

## 4. The Debunk

The perfect-register claim is unsupported. Uncertainty about a precise total is not denial of catastrophic scale. Equally, a plausible estimate should not be repeated without a source and definition. The event's gravity does not depend on the historian pretending that its destruction somehow left an exhaustive count behind.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Use sourced estimates and state the scope. Keep death and displacement distinct. When a number is not necessary to the point, describe the established experience rather than importing unsourced arithmetic. A careful account can make the disaster comprehensible without presenting an invented precision as a form of respect.

# Claim 09: Punjabi belongs exclusively to Sikhs

Punjabi is the language of only one religion, so anyone outside that religion using or claiming it is borrowing another community's property.

## 1. Where It Came From

Language and script become symbols in political conflict. A strong association is converted into exclusive ownership. Gurmukhi's significance in Sikh scripture is then treated as proof that Punjabi has no Muslim or Hindu speakers, no Shahmukhi tradition and no shared social life beyond the faith.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The Guru Granth Sahib uses linguistic resources from a wide world and includes contributors of varied backgrounds. That fact should encourage careful description of language, not a claim that scripture creates exclusive ownership of all speech in a region. Script, language, literary tradition and religious identity must be distinguished. [MANN-NANAK]

## 3. The Real History

Punjabi has been spoken and written across religious communities and across the later India–Pakistan border. Scripts and institutional status differ. Political language declarations can also differ from everyday speech. To assess a census claim, examine its question, classification and circumstances instead of assuming every answer is a transparent recording of home conversation.

## 4. The Debunk

Exclusive religious ownership is false. Sikh literary and institutional contributions remain substantial without excluding other Punjabi traditions. Nor does sharing a language erase religious differences. The ordinary capacity to speak to a neighbour does not require everyone to possess the same theology, citizenship or choice of script.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Recognise Punjabi's shared life and describe the particular tradition being discussed. A language can carry a community's scripture and also belong to speakers outside the community. The reader gains more from understanding scripts, migration and education than from being instructed to treat a familiar word as someone else's exclusive property.

# Claim 10: Punjabi Suba and Khalistan have always been the same demand

Every demand for a Punjabi-speaking Indian state was necessarily a demand for an independent Sikh state, so no distinction in goal or period matters.

## 1. Where It Came From

Later conflict is projected backwards. The shared presence of Sikh political organisations is treated as proof that every programme had the same constitutional objective. Language reorganisation, federal autonomy and independence are then collapsed into one demand, which makes it unnecessary to read what a particular movement actually requested.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Scripture does not supply the constitutional programme of every modern party. The use of religious institutions in mobilisation may be relevant to a political history, but it cannot settle the text of a demand. A political claim should be read in its document, date and organisational setting rather than deduced from a religious label.

## 3. The Real History

The Punjabi Suba movement concerned state reorganisation, and a smaller Punjab and Haryana emerged in 1966. Later demands for autonomy and for independence had different formulations and contexts. Participants' aims could differ, but a claim of identical objectives needs evidence of those objectives, not simply the fact that Sikh actors were involved in both periods.

## 4. The Debunk

The automatic identity of the demands is false. It does not follow that language and community politics were unrelated, or that every supporter had only one motive. It means that constitutional outcomes, stated goals and political rhetoric must be compared rather than merged. An accusation is not a substitute for the movement's actual programme.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Distinguish a state within India, changes in federal powers and a proposed independent state. Explain where actors and arguments overlap and where they do not. Readers can then evaluate the politics rather than inherit a label that has already decided the answer. A later dispute should not erase the meaning of an earlier demand.

# Closing

The thread through this part is agency under changing institutions. Colonial power mattered. Indian reformers argued and organised. Legal categories affected representation. Community boundaries could be meaningful and still change. Partition brought unequal, specific responsibilities and suffering that no complete-community label can fairly contain.
