# Foreword

A line of Gurbani can travel farther in a day than the whole shabad travels in a reader's lifetime. It arrives in a screenshot, under a photograph, above an argument. Its author may be misidentified. Its qualifying line may be missing. Its translation may contain a modern idea that the original does not state. And because it looks scriptural, people stop asking the ordinary questions they would ask of another quotation.

This part is about reading the book rather than winning with a fragment of it. The Guru Granth Sahib matters as scripture, as the recognised Guru in Sikh life and as a historically transmitted text. Those are related descriptions, but each calls for a different kind of care. History investigates compilation and manuscripts. Interpretation investigates wording and context. Religious life investigates what it means to receive the Shabad as authority. None requires pretending that every printed caption is accurate.

The ang numbers used here refer to the standard printed arrangement. They help locate a passage; they are not a claim that every historical manuscript had that pagination. My English explanations are paraphrases, not replacements for the original. Read the heading, the surrounding composition and the rahao where present. Where interpretation is disputed, compare the arguments rather than treating the first English result as a final court of appeal.

The extended readings at the end draw on my existing Punjab research. They provide fuller context for the compilation, its contributors and the community in which the book became authoritative. They are identified as earlier writing, so the reader can distinguish the new claim-by-claim examination from the historical background it uses. Original testimony, modern scholarship and my own previous explanation should never be mistaken for three independent witnesses merely because all three appear in the notes.

# Claim 01: All Ten Gurus Wrote the Guru Granth Sahib

The claim assumes that ten recognised Gurus must mean ten contributing authors inside the scripture, and that nobody else could have a scriptural voice.

## 1. Where It Came From

An introductory shorthand becomes a factual statement. People hear scripture of the Sikh Gurus and expand it to every Guru wrote it. The mistake also arises when religious authority, authorship and compilation are treated as one role. A person can transmit or confer authority on a work without composing its entire contents.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The standard text identifies compositions by headings such as Mahalla 1 and Mahalla 5 and by named Bhagat sections. Mahalla identifies the Guru's place in succession, not ten authors gathered in every section. Compare Asa at ang 473 with the Bhagat Kabir section in Prabhati at ang 1349. [SGGS473] [SGGS1349]

## 3. The Real History

The received canonical collection contains bani of six Sikh Gurus, together with other contributors, including Bhagats and Bhatts. Guru Arjan's compilation and the later recognition associated with Guru Gobind Singh are different stages. Count the named authorial headings rather than inferring a contributor list from the number of Gurus. [MANN-EARLY]

## 4. The Debunk

All ten wrote it is false as stated. Nobody except the Gurus contributed is also false. Neither correction lowers the spiritual standing of a Guru who has no separately attributed composition in this canon. Authorship is a textual fact; Guruship is not a prize for publishing a minimum number of pages.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Say that the scripture includes the compositions of six Gurus and other recognised contributors, and identify the particular author being read. The selected inclusion of other voices is a substantive feature of the book. It cannot be appreciated if a false contributor list hides it.

# Claim 02: The 1604 Adi Granth Was Already Identical to Every Modern Printed Volume

The claim conflates the authoritative compilation made under Guru Arjan Dev Ji with the final received corpus, its subsequent transmission and its standard modern typography.

## 1. Where It Came From

The word original is doing too much work. It may mean the first authorised compilation, a preserved manuscript, the received canon or a familiar printed edition. A speaker moves between these meanings without warning and makes a changing physical history sound like one unchanged object.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The standard volume includes saloks attributed to the ninth Guru near its conclusion. Guru Tegh Bahadur Sahib Ji lived after the 1604 compilation. The existence of those compositions alone makes it necessary to distinguish stages rather than treating the first compilation and the final received corpus as identical in every respect. [SGGS1429]

## 3. The Real History

Compilation in 1604, incorporation of later bani and recognition of the Guru Granth Sahib's authority are historically connected but distinct. The institutional narrative and manuscript scholarship differ over some reconstruction details. The fact that a process had stages is not evidence that its outcome was casually assembled or lacked authoritative oversight. [SGPC-GG] [MANN]

## 4. The Debunk

The literal identity claim fails. It is also wrong to infer that a later stage means wholesale invention. Identify what changed: corpus, arrangement, pagination, spacing or physical format. These are different questions, and a difference in one cannot automatically be used to announce a difference in all the others.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Give the compilation its proper historical importance without asking it to contain words written by a later Guru before he lived. A history of preservation can include authoritative development. The argument is more credible when its stages are named instead of being hidden beneath a comforting but impossible simplification.

# Claim 03: The Number 1430 Was the Physical Page Count of Every Early Manuscript

The claim treats the ang numbering of the standard volume as an ancient invariant of every handwritten copy, regardless of size, handwriting or layout.

## 1. Where It Came From

A modern citation system becomes part of imagined original manufacture. Because readers reliably find a line at a numbered ang today, they assume every scribe used the same number of lines, the same paper and the same page divisions. A useful location system is being mistaken for evidence about every old object.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The standard text extends through ang 1430. That is a reliable way to locate its received arrangement. The last sections, including the transition into Ragmala near ang 1429, can be checked directly. The location does not identify the folio numbering of a differently formatted manuscript. [SGGS1429]

## 3. The Real History

Handwritten books vary in dimensions, writing density, foliation and ancillary material. Manuscript researchers compare content and sequence rather than assuming that a modern page reference is also a manuscript description. A catalogue should identify the particular object, date, folios and relevant passage; a printed ang reference is an additional aid.

## 4. The Debunk

The universal physical-page claim is false. This correction does not challenge the established printed numbering or the reverential word ang. It prevents an anachronism: a reader must not use the modern count as proof that a purported early manuscript is authentic or false without examining the object itself.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Use ang 1430 for the standard received edition and folio references when discussing a manuscript. Tell readers which format is under examination. That small discipline makes citations more useful and helps stop photographs of isolated pages being used to support claims about a book nobody has actually identified.

# Claim 04: Every Manuscript Difference Proves Deliberate Corruption

The claim moves from any variant spelling, marginal note, order or added item to a charge that an enemy intentionally destroyed the scripture's integrity.

## 1. Where It Came From

Threat changes the emotional meaning of a difference. A visible variation feels like an attack before its type has been identified. The opposite temptation is to deny all differences because admitting one appears dangerous. Both reactions make it harder to do the comparison that would establish whether a variation is significant.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The received scripture provides the text with which a community reads and compares. It does not label every historical scribe's intention. A mistake, correction, variant and unauthorised addition must be distinguished through evidence about the manuscript, not diagnosed from a verse chosen to condemn deceit. [MANN-EARLY]

## 3. The Real History

Textual study examines the relationships between copies and the formation of recognised versions. Scholars disagree about some early witnesses and their interpretation. Their disagreement concerns actual material, not a choice between everything was perfect and everything was invented. A copy can contain an error while faithfully transmitting most of a work. [MANN-NANAK]

## 4. The Debunk

A difference does not prove a motive. To establish deliberate alteration, identify the change, its chronology, the relevant person or group and evidence of intention. To establish an important textual consequence, show the reading it changes. The charge should be as specific as the evidence, not enlarged into a universal conspiracy.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Preservation is a practice carried out by people: collecting, checking, correcting, teaching and transmitting. Acknowledging that work can make respect for the canon more concrete. We do not have to deny the labour of preservation to value its result, and we should not confuse the presence of labour with proof of fraud.

# Claim 05: Ragmala Has Never Been Disputed Within the Panth

The claim says that all Sikh traditions have always held exactly the same position about Ragmala, its authorship and its use at the conclusion of a reading.

## 1. Where It Came From

A local convention can feel universal when a person encounters no other one. In a heated dispute, that experience becomes a claim that all dissent is new or hostile. The history of actual disagreement disappears, and an inherited practice is presented as something no Sikh could ever have questioned.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Ragmala is printed at the end of the standard volume. The modern Sikh Rehat Maryada's Article XI explicitly accommodates disagreement about whether a reading concludes at Mundavani or includes Ragmala, while prohibiting printing a volume without it. That distinction should be read before accusing another congregation of inventing its convention. [SGGS1429] [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

The dispute involves arguments about manuscripts, attribution, musical description and received practice. Presence in an edition, authorship and liturgical reading are different matters. A researcher must identify which is being discussed and consult evidence for that question. One fact cannot silently settle every other issue.

## 4. The Debunk

Never disputed is false. The communal code itself records a practical response to disagreement. That does not mean that every historical claim made by either side is correct. Acknowledging a dispute requires presenting arguments accurately, not awarding each participant an equal share of factual truth regardless of evidence.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Describe the recognised printed text, the convention being followed and the dispute separately. The institutional arrangement is an example of managing disagreement without pretending it never existed. A reader can understand that arrangement without being forced to take a side through a slogan or a threat.

# Claim 06: Every English Translation Is the Same as the Original Bani

The claim treats a translator's word choice as if it were the original text speaking directly in contemporary English, with no interpretation between them.

## 1. Where It Came From

Accessibility can hide mediation. An English sentence reads smoothly, so the reader forgets that someone selected its vocabulary, grammar and theological emphasis. Search results can then place several translations beside each other without making their differences visible. Fluency becomes the appearance of certainty.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The original employs a multilingual literary vocabulary and poetic forms. Words such as hukam and naam carry contexts that no single English substitute fully reproduces. At the opening of Japji Sahib, compare the original wording and several explanations before concluding that a modern philosophical term is the only possible reading. [SGGS1]

## 3. The Real History

Translation is part of a continuing interpretive tradition. It can be careful, useful and still contestable. A translator may make a context explicit, compress an image or choose between plausible senses. To assess a disputed rendering, identify the original construction, the translator and the grounds for the choice.

## 4. The Debunk

A translation is not a replacement autograph. Disagreement among English renderings does not itself prove that the original is meaningless or unreliable. It proves that a translation decision must be examined. Conversely, an impressive-looking English quotation cannot establish a scriptural claim if its key term has no demonstrated basis in the original.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Use translations to enter the text, then keep their status visible. Say my paraphrase when paraphrasing and name the translator when quoting. A reader who knows a little less Punjabi should still be given enough information to ask an intelligent question rather than being told to accept an English sentence on authority.

# Claim 07: The Closing Name Nanak Identifies Guru Nanak as the Author Every Time

The claim assumes that the signature Nanak means that no other Guru can have composed the hymn, regardless of the printed heading.

## 1. Where It Came From

Readers apply the normal rule of personal signatures to a tradition with a succession expressed through a shared name. A screenshot containing the closing line omits the heading, and the missing information seems unnecessary because the name looks familiar. The mistake is especially easy when a translator suppresses structural labels.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Mahalla headings distinguish the Gurus while compositions may close in the name Nanak. A shabad marked Mahalla 5 should not be reclassified as Mahalla 1 merely because its closing uses Nanak. Read the heading as part of the text's attribution system. The Kanara section at ang 1299 offers a direct example. [SGGS1299]

## 3. The Real History

Sikh succession is expressed through continuity as well as particular authorship. The textual heading makes the particularity visible. Removing it creates a false choice between shared Guruship and distinct compositional voices. We do not need to choose: the tradition's own apparatus supplies both.

## 4. The Debunk

The automatic attribution is false. A personal-name shortcut cannot override an explicit authorial heading. The same care applies to quoted speech and invoked names within a composition: the person named in a line is not always the person responsible for the whole passage.

## 5. The Honest Picture

A useful citation gives the work, raag or section, Mahalla or contributor and ang. That makes verification possible for another reader. Attribution is not a minor courtesy; it affects the historical question being asked and stops a quotation from being used as evidence about the wrong lifetime.

# Claim 08: Na Hum Hindu Na Musalman Needs No Attribution Note

The claim says that one famous line can be cited simply as Kabir, or simply as an isolated modern declaration, without checking the heading or the surrounding shabad.

## 1. Where It Came From

The closing invocation of Kabir encourages a straightforward Bhagat Kabir attribution. The earlier part of this series used that shorthand. A current reading must make the textual complication visible rather than repeating it because correcting a familiar attribution feels embarrassing.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

On ang 1136 the shabad is printed in Bhairao under Mahalla 5, and its closing invokes Kabir. Both features are present. The safe citation reproduces both rather than deleting the heading to preserve a favourite author label. The surrounding lines discuss worship, fasting and the one divine reality. [SGGS1136]

## 3. The Real History

There are interpretive discussions about how the Kabir invocation relates to the Mahalla heading. This part does not pretend that reading an English website settles that scholarship. It does establish a directly inspectable textual fact: the printed heading must be included in a responsible attribution note.

## 4. The Debunk

The earlier unqualified shorthand needs qualification. That does not make the line disappear or settle every modern identity argument. It corrects the precision of the citation and reduces the temptation to make a single sentence carry a complete account of Sikh identity across five centuries.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Read the whole shabad and cite Bhairao, Mahalla 5, ang 1136, noting the Kabir invocation. This is a concrete correction within my own series. The method has little worth if it applies to somebody else's careless screenshot but refuses to correct the label beneath my own epigraph.

# Claim 09: Including Hindu and Muslim Contributors Makes Every Doctrine Interchangeable

The claim turns the inclusion of Bhagat bani into a declaration that every religious teaching, ritual and institution has exactly the same meaning.

## 1. Where It Came From

Inclusion is confused with indiscriminate approval. A modern reader may want a simple harmony statement, while a hostile reader may want to deny Sikh distinctness. Both can use a contributor's background as a shortcut around the actual content and selection of the compositions.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The book contains distinct voices and critiques. Kabir's Prabhati shabads challenge ways of locating the divine and the hypocrisy of religious practice. Inclusion does not erase the criticism. A composition must be read for what it affirms and contests, rather than turned into a certificate approving every doctrine associated with its author's community. [SGGS1349] [SGGS1350]

## 3. The Real History

Compilation was selective. A canon can recognise insight beyond a communal boundary while maintaining its own commitments. The compiler's choice is an act of judgement, not a random gathering of agreeable names. The historical background reading below explains why contributors and selection matter together.

## 4. The Debunk

Interchangeability does not follow from inclusion. Neither does exclusion of all outside insight follow from Sikh distinctness. The false alternatives disappear once we examine actual compositions: a tradition can receive a voice, interpret it and reject some practices associated with the wider world from which it came.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Respect another person's religious identity without pretending that disagreement has ceased to exist. The scripture gives reason to recognise shared human worth and to examine teachings seriously. It does not require replacing actual religions with a vague sameness that their adherents may not recognise.

# Claim 10: Respect for the Guru Granth Sahib Makes Understanding Optional

The claim says that correct external handling and a respectful appearance fully discharge the reader's responsibility, even when the teaching is never understood or considered in conduct.

## 1. Where It Came From

Visible actions are easier to standardise and observe than attention and ethical change. A community can therefore become excellent at protecting the physical volume while neglecting the difficult work of reading. Criticising that imbalance is sometimes mistaken for opposing respectful handling itself.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Truthful conduct is emphasised in Siri Raag, ang 62. The opening of Japji Sahib asks how one becomes truthful; it does not present the question as solved merely by owning a book. These are interpretive resources for asking whether reverence reaches the life of the person offering it. [SGGS62] [SGGS1]

## 3. The Real History

The book's musical organisation, congregation, teaching and commentary are part of the world in which it is received. Reading and singing are not decorative activities attached to a silent object. They bring the teaching into a community that must interpret it and decide what obedience would actually mean.

## 4. The Debunk

Understanding is not made irrelevant by physical reverence. The opposite claim, that external care is inherently pointless, also overreaches. A sacred object's respectful handling can express a real commitment. The mistake is treating that expression as proof that the commitment has already transformed behaviour.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Keep respectful handling and meaningful study together. A beginner can start with one complete shabad, a careful explanation and a question about conduct. The goal is neither to rush into expertise nor to remain permanently dependent on captions. A teaching becomes more available when the reader is encouraged to understand it.

# Closing

The ten claims have one common failure: they use a small visible feature to replace a larger task. A shared signature replaces an authorial heading. A page number replaces a manuscript history. A single line replaces a shabad. A contributor's background replaces the content chosen for the canon. Respectful handling replaces the question of what a teaching demands.

The remedy is a reading habit. Begin with the original location. Record the heading. Read before and after the line. Distinguish quotation, paraphrase and inference. If the claim concerns a manuscript, ask for the particular object and folio. If it concerns religious authority, identify the community's recognised teaching rather than smuggling a historical assertion into a devotional statement.
