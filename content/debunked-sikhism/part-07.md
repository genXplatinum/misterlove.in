# Foreword

Most people meet religion through ordinary things: a morning prayer, a meal, a visit to a gurdwara, a wedding, a family rule. Those things can carry a teaching. They can also collect promises that the teaching did not make. Pay for this reading and the problem will disappear. Do this number of repetitions and a business will succeed. Keep this outward appearance and your behaviour no longer needs examination.

I am not writing a replacement conduct code. This part compares common claims with the actual institutional code and with the ethical language of Gurbani. The distinction is essential. A rule about what a community ought to do is not a survey proving what every member does. A religious expression of trust is not a measurable guarantee of a particular result. A local convention is not automatically the practice of every Sikh in every place.

Practice becomes intelligible when its purpose is explained. The point is not to remove every shared form in the name of simplicity. It is to stop treating a form as though it were a machine. Discipline, understanding, service and responsibility belong together. A reader should not have to choose between an empty performance and a life with no common practices at all.

# Claim 01: Rejecting empty ritual means Sikhi has no prescribed practices

Because the Gurus criticise ritual without understanding, a Sikh has no prescribed prayer, ceremony, discipline or collective observance.

## 1. Where It Came From

Criticism of mechanical religion is shortened to two words: no rituals. The phrase is then used to dismiss any structured practice someone dislikes. A critic can use the same phrase to call every Sikh ceremony a contradiction. Both arguments depend on using ritual first to mean empty performance and then to mean any repeated or shared action.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Siri Raag, ang 62, makes truthful conduct central. That is not a command to abolish the congregation. The Sikh Rehat Maryada describes daily recitation, congregational worship, reading, initiation and marriage. Those prescriptions show that criticism of hollow performance does not equal the absence of discipline or ceremony. [SGGS62] [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

Sikh communities developed institutions through successive Gurus: places of gathering, organised teaching, shared meals and arrangements for transmitting scripture. The form and administration of these institutions changed over time. A historical account must ask which practice existed in which period, rather than treating all structure as a later departure from an imagined structureless beginning.

## 4. The Debunk

The blanket claim is false. A practice can be prescribed without being understood as a purchase of divine favour. The relevant criticism concerns what people believe the action does and how it relates to conduct. An identical outward action can be approached as learning, shared discipline, status display or a transaction; that difference deserves explanation.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Ask why a practice is done, where its prescription comes from and whether it carries the teaching into life. Do not let a slogan remove those questions. A coherent account of Sikhi can recognise its organised practices and still challenge the belief that performing them excuses cruelty, dishonesty or neglect.

# Claim 02: An Akhand Path purchases a guaranteed outcome

Paying for a continuous reading of scripture guarantees that an illness will end, a court case will be won or a family problem will disappear.

## 1. Where It Came From

A reading accompanies a difficult time. A hoped-for result follows, and the sequence becomes a promise. Sometimes an advertised service strengthens the promise with a price and a deadline. Gratitude for a meaningful experience is then turned into a contract that is supposedly binding on the divine.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The code describes an Akhand Path as continuous reading and specifies arrangements for beginning and concluding it. It also expects careful reading. Those instructions concern an observance, not a schedule of guaranteed medical, financial or legal results. The existence of a prescribed reading does not authenticate an extra promise made by its seller. [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

Readings can accompany celebration, mourning, remembrance and collective commitment. To claim a particular causal effect, however, requires evidence for that effect. A family remembering a recovery after a reading is evidence of the family's experience and interpretation. It is not a controlled comparison establishing that the reading cured the illness.

## 4. The Debunk

The guaranteed outcome is unsupported. This does not make the reading meaningless. It removes an unjustified promise, especially one that may pressure a frightened person to spend money or blame themselves when suffering continues. Neither the number of listeners nor the reputation of a service provider turns a promise into demonstrated causation.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Explain the observance as reading, listening and collective participation. Make time for understanding rather than treating completion as a receipt. Anyone offering an outcome guarantee should be asked for the basis of that claim. A person facing illness or danger also needs the appropriate practical help; religious participation does not erase that need.

# Claim 03: A Hukamnama is a personalised fortune prediction

The Hukam read at a gurdwara predicts an individual's exact future and removes the need to make a responsible decision.

## 1. Where It Came From

A line feels relevant to a private worry. Relevance becomes a prediction, and a prediction becomes a command about a job, marriage or investment. The person interpreting it may add details that are absent from the shabad. Because the reading is sacred, those added details can become difficult to question.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The code's Article VII sets out how the complete hymn is selected and read, including beginning on the preceding page when necessary. That instruction works against extracting whichever fragment serves a preferred answer. Article XVI rejects omens and horoscopic dispositions. These are important checks on converting scripture into fortune-telling. [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

A congregation receives the Hukam together. Individuals can reflect on how its teaching bears on their circumstances. Reflection does not establish that a verse contains an unprinted prediction of next week's exam score. To test a prediction, it would need to be stated clearly before the event and evaluated beyond successful anecdotes.

## 4. The Debunk

The exact-future claim adds information the text does not supply. It also lets an interpreter borrow the authority of scripture for their own advice. Guidance can shape a person's priorities without settling every factual detail of a decision. The distinction matters most when someone is asked to surrender money, consent or medical care.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Read the whole shabad, study its meaning and identify the ethical guidance you take from it. Then examine the actual circumstances of the decision. A sacred reading is not less meaningful because a person still checks the facts, considers another person's rights and accepts responsibility for the choice they make.

# Claim 04: Naam is a formula for guaranteed wealth

Repeating a divine name a specified number of times guarantees money, promotion or success, regardless of conduct and circumstances.

## 1. Where It Came From

An instruction for remembrance becomes a numerical recipe. Numbers sound precise, so a claim about success appears more trustworthy when it includes an exact count or a deadline. A seller may promise results while explaining every failure as inadequate faith. That creates a promise the seller never has to let evidence disprove.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Japji Sahib begins with the problem of becoming truthful and living within Hukam. Sarang, ang 1245, connects work and sharing with the path. These are not instructions to bypass work, generosity or judgement through an exact recitation total. Naam belongs to a religious account of remembrance and transformation, not to a guaranteed income calculator. [SGGS1] [SGGS1245]

## 3. The Real History

Disciplined recitation can structure attention, community and daily life. Whether it affects a particular commercial outcome is a separate claim. Business success also involves demand, skill, capital, labour, luck and other people's choices. Selecting prosperous practitioners while ignoring struggling ones would not isolate an effect of the practice.

## 4. The Debunk

The material guarantee has not been established by the religious instruction. It is particularly cruel to imply that poverty, unemployment or bereavement proves a person remembered the divine incorrectly. A tradition's account of grace cannot honestly be reduced to a promise that every devout person's life will be financially easier than everyone else's.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Describe remembrance in its religious terms and make practical claims separately. A person can pray and prepare for an interview. They can practise devotion and pay workers fairly. Neither activity needs the invention of a universal promise that the first will always make the second profitable.

# Claim 05: Serving Langar cancels exploitation elsewhere

A person who donates to or serves in Langar has fulfilled equality, even if they mistreat employees or practise discrimination outside the gurdwara.

## 1. Where It Came From

A visible act of service becomes the whole moral assessment of a person. Public generosity is easy to photograph; private employment practices are harder to see. The meal then becomes an alibi. A donor's contribution is counted carefully while the people who may have paid for it through underpaid labour disappear from the story.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Sarang, ang 1245, links earning through work with sharing. Kanara, ang 1299, refuses the enemy-and-stranger view of humanity. These teachings do not divide life into a sacred hour in which equality matters and an ordinary week in which it does not. A shared meal can enact a principle whose demands continue afterwards. [SGGS1245] [SGGS1299]

## 3. The Real History

Langar is an institution, not only a private generous feeling. Food must be obtained, cooked, served and cleaned up. Access and dignity matter at each stage. Sitting together challenges a hierarchy, but it does not by itself establish equal access to land, marriage, employment or decision-making in the wider community.

## 4. The Debunk

Service is not a moral cancellation policy. A good act remains good without making other acts harmless. The critic who dismisses all Langar because discrimination persists also goes too far. The proper question is how the institution works and whether those who support it carry its implications into their other relationships.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Value the food and labour actually offered. Then ask whether workers are paid, participants are respected and people facing exclusion can raise a concern. A meal's equality is more convincing when it strengthens a habit of equal treatment than when it serves as the reason nobody may ask about inequality anywhere else.

# Claim 06: Dasvandh is either buying merit or only handing cash to one institution

Giving a portion of one's resources purchases forgiveness, and it counts only if a particular organisation receives the money.

## 1. Where It Came From

A discipline of giving is translated into an institutional sales claim. The amount is visible, so it becomes easier to compare than the origin of the money or the effect of the donation. Someone can be pressured to pay first and understand later. At its worst, the transaction promises absolution while avoiding accountability.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The relevant scriptural emphasis is work and sharing in Sarang, ang 1245. The code connects conduct with honest livelihood and care for people in need. A donation made from exploitative earnings does not make the exploitation disappear. No passage cited here establishes that one present organisation is the only possible recipient of meaningful giving. [SGGS1245] [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

Communities have needed resources for gathering, teaching, food and assistance. That practical need makes administration and trust important. A donor should be able to ask where money goes. A charitable label is not evidence of transparent accounts, and an organisation's large membership does not establish that every use of its funds is wise.

## 4. The Debunk

Giving does not purchase the right to mistreat people. The claim of an exclusive present recipient needs its own evidence. Equally, calling all organised fundraising a fraud would erase genuine communal work. Evaluate the actual institution: its purpose, accounts, decision-making and treatment of those it says it serves.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Make giving deliberate, accountable and proportionate to real responsibilities. Explain the religious discipline without adding a magical return on investment. Support useful work with clear knowledge of the recipient. The question is what the resources do for people, not only whose name appears beside the largest number.

# Claim 07: Initiation makes later misconduct impossible

An Amritdhari Sikh is automatically incapable of lying, abusing others or acting unjustly, so an accusation against an initiated person need not be examined.

## 1. Where It Came From

A serious commitment is treated as a permanent certificate of character. People mistake a vow about conduct for evidence that conduct never fails. Institutions may then protect a respected figure's reputation before hearing the person reporting harm. An outward marker becomes a substitute for investigating an action.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The code prescribes responsibilities for initiated Sikhs and procedures concerning breaches. Its existence makes little sense if initiation made every breach impossible. Siri Raag, ang 62, likewise directs attention to truthful living. A commitment establishes what a person undertakes, not an exemption from evidence about what they subsequently do. [SRM] [SGGS62]

## 3. The Real History

Religious communities consist of human beings with different conduct, opportunities and levels of power. A title, office or discipline can provide reasons for expecting responsibility; it cannot guarantee it. A credible assessment of a complaint requires the relevant facts and fair procedure rather than automatic belief based on either the person's appearance or the accuser's identity.

## 4. The Debunk

The immunity claim is false. The opposite generalisation that one offender proves all initiated Sikhs hypocritical is false as well. Responsibility belongs to actual actions and accountable institutions. Treating a discipline as meaningful means taking breaches seriously rather than explaining them away to protect the image of the discipline.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Respect a commitment and still investigate misconduct. Protect access to fair reporting, especially where the person accused holds authority. Do not turn outward appearance into a reason to silence a victim, and do not turn an individual case into a reason to condemn everyone who shares that appearance.

# Claim 08: There is one undisputed Sikh answer to every meat question

Every Sikh community has always followed exactly the same dietary rule, and one cropped verse settles every dispute about meat.

## 1. Where It Came From

Food marks identity strongly. A family's rule feels universal because it is familiar. Another person finds a verse criticising a food argument and treats it as permission for every possible choice. The debate then moves between scripture, initiation rules, Langar menus and personal diet without acknowledging that these are different questions.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Malar, ang 1289, contains Guru Nanak's criticism of a reductive flesh debate. Read the sequence rather than extracting a permission slip or prohibition from a fragment. The code's initiation rules identify specified serious breaches, including the category it calls kuthha. The precise rule and the interpretation a community gives it should be stated rather than hidden. [SGGS1289] [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

Different Sikh traditions and individuals follow different dietary disciplines. A vegetarian shared meal also serves the practical purpose of broad access across dietary commitments. That does not demonstrate that every diner follows the same diet at home. Historical claims about diet require dated practices and witnesses, not a menu photographed today.

## 4. The Debunk

The claim of complete and permanent agreement is inaccurate. Scriptural criticism of pride does not automatically settle every institutional rule, and a stricter institutional rule does not prove that all other Sikhs follow it. Nor should a diet be advertised as nutritionally complete without examining what is actually eaten.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Identify the scripture, the current code and the particular tradition separately. Follow the discipline you undertake without pretending the history contains no disagreement. Discuss nutrition as nutrition and ethics as ethics. A useful conversation about food requires more than calling everyone with a different practice either ignorant or impure.

# Claim 09: A religious rule against intoxicants means prescribed treatment must stop

Because Sikh discipline rejects intoxicants, a person should refuse any prescribed medicine that affects the nervous system or contains a substance used differently for intoxication.

## 1. Where It Came From

A prohibition directed at intoxication becomes a homemade medical rule. Drug names or ingredients sound frightening, and a general religious sentence is used to override diagnosis, dose and supervised use. The person giving the instruction may know neither the condition nor the consequences of suddenly stopping treatment.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Article XVI lists intoxicants within the code's conduct rules. It does not provide a clinical prescribing manual for every disease and medicine. The difference between recreational intoxication and an indicated, supervised treatment must not be erased through a caption. This chapter cannot confer clinical clearance or replace a clinician's assessment. [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

Medical treatment is evaluated for a condition, a patient, a dose, potential interactions and evidence of benefit and harm. A medicine's use under supervision cannot be inferred from a story about misuse of another substance. Equally, a label calling something natural, spiritual or traditional does not establish that it is safe.

## 4. The Debunk

The instruction to stop prescribed care does not follow from the cited rule. A religious concern should be discussed with a qualified clinician and, where useful, a trusted knowledgeable religious adviser. Abrupt withdrawal or delay may be dangerous. No anonymous religious or medical caption deserves authority to make that decision for a patient.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Keep the commitment against intoxication and seek competent care. Explain concerns about ingredients or effects so alternatives can be assessed safely. Do not stop or change treatment on this chapter's authority. Serious discipline includes accepting the limits of one's own expertise, especially when another person's health is involved.

# Claim 10: Different festival dates prove the historical event is false

If two Sikh calendars observe a Gurpurab on different civil dates, the Guru's life or the commemorated event must have been invented.

## 1. Where It Came From

People expect a birthday to correspond to one familiar date each year. A lunar date, a solar calendar and a civil-calendar conversion are treated as though they were the same system. Institutional disagreement about observance is then turned into a verdict about whether anything happened at all.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The code rejects dependence on auspicious and inauspicious dates for decisions such as marriage. That is different from denying the need for a calendar to arrange a shared commemoration. A scripture's ethical instruction cannot alone establish the historical conversion of a seventeenth-century date into a present civil calendar. [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

Dates can be expressed in different calendars, with different starts to the year and different rules for months. Sikh institutions also make choices about collective observance. SGPC publishes an annual calendar; other communities may use a different system or set of institutional decisions. The existence of a published calendar does not settle every debate about conversion. [SGPC-CALENDAR]

## 4. The Debunk

Different observance dates do not demonstrate an invented life. They may reflect conversion, calendar reform or a dispute over commemorative practice. The correct historical question asks for the earliest evidence and its calendar. The correct scheduling question asks which calendar the organising institution has adopted for that year.

## 5. The Honest Picture

State both the original calendar date, where known, and the conversion method when precision matters. For attendance, use the organiser's current notice. Do not pretend that a remembered event depends on one calendar argument being settled, and do not conceal genuine disagreement beneath an unjustified claim of complete unanimity.

# Closing

These ten claims make the same substitution: a religious practice is asked to do something that its existence does not establish. A reading becomes a medical guarantee. Guidance becomes fortune-telling. Service becomes a cancellation of exploitation. A vow becomes immunity from accountability. A calendar dispute becomes evidence that a life never happened.

The answer is to recover the actual purpose and limits of the practice. Study and respect can strengthen each other. Shared forms can make teaching accessible. They become misleading when an added promise is protected from examination because it stands beside something sacred.

The following readings adapt my earlier chapters on Kartarpur, the institutions developed by successive Gurus and Amritsar. They explain why worship, organisation and shared meals cannot be separated into a pure idea on one side and disposable machinery on the other. They are identified background explanations, not proof that every modern practice has one unchanged origin.
