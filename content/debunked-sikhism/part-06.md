# Foreword

One argument has swallowed an unreasonable amount of oxygen: either accept every page of a large collection as the work of one author, or admit that you have rejected the tenth Guru. That is a demand for allegiance disguised as a question about books. A manuscript cannot be dated by measuring the loyalty of the person looking at it.

I want to separate three questions. Who composed a particular work? How was it collected and transmitted? What authority does a community give it? The answers can overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A prayer can have an established place in worship while scholars debate the history of the collection in which it appears. A work can be old without being the Guru Granth Sahib. A character can speak inside a story without the author commanding the reader to imitate that character.

The Dasam Granth is associated with Guru Gobind Singh Ji and contains different kinds of writing. Debate over authorship and compilation is real. This chapter does not pretend to resolve every manuscript problem. It shows why the loudest shortcuts do not resolve them either. The standard throughout is specific: name the composition, the witness, the passage and the question. A whole book cannot be checked through one cropped line. [MANN]

# Claim 01: One answer settles the authorship of the entire Dasam Granth

Every composition in the Dasam Granth has exactly the same authorship evidence, so one accepted passage proves the author of every other passage.

## 1. Where It Came From

The collection's title and its association with the tenth Guru make a single-author answer feel natural. In an argument, that answer saves time: someone establishes the standing of a familiar prayer and treats the rest as already settled. Critics sometimes make the reverse move, using one disputed portion to dismiss the entire collection. Both moves ignore what a collection is.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The Guru Granth Sahib is identified as Guru in the Sikh Rehat Maryada. The same code prescribes particular compositions associated with Guru Gobind Singh in daily observance and initiation. Those are specific prescriptions. They do not say that every separate question of compilation and manuscript history has one evidential answer. Read the prescriptions in Articles IV and XXIV alongside the definition in Article I. [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

The collection contains devotional, autobiographical, martial, narrative and Persian materials. Scholars examine manuscripts, arrangement, dates, signatures, language and relationships between texts. Mann's study is an accessible starting point for the diversity of early sources. A date attached to one manuscript establishes something about that physical witness; it does not automatically date the original composition of everything it contains. [MANN]

## 4. The Debunk

The proposed shortcut fails. A collection must be examined composition by composition, and sometimes passage by passage. This does not prove that a disputed work is inauthentic. It tells us that authenticity cannot be transferred from its neighbour like a property certificate. Nor does a difficult passage cancel the evidence for better-attested material elsewhere.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Say what is accepted, by whom, and on what grounds. If a claim concerns Jaap Sahib, discuss Jaap Sahib. If it concerns Charitropakhyan, discuss that work and its witnesses. A reader deserves the actual argument rather than a demand to choose between two slogans about thousands of lines.

# Claim 02: Liturgical use makes the Dasam Granth a second Guru Granth Sahib

If compositions from a book are used in Sikh prayer, that entire book must have exactly the same canonical and institutional status as the Guru Granth Sahib.

## 1. Where It Came From

People see a composition recited in a central ceremony and infer that its containing volume must be another enthroned Guru. Others fear that accepting the prayer would necessarily accept that inference. The dispute begins with an assumption that all kinds of religious authority must be identical. Traditions usually distinguish them more carefully.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The code names the Guru Granth Sahib separately and identifies particular prayers for particular observances. Japji Sahib belongs to the Guru Granth Sahib; Jaap Sahib and the prescribed Savaiye belong to a different textual setting. The initiation prescription brings compositions together for a ceremony without collapsing their containing works into one canonical status. [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

Reception varies among institutions and Sikh traditions. Practices of displaying, reading and honouring texts need to be identified at the particular place concerned. An observed practice at one shrine proves that the practice occurs there. It is not, by itself, evidence that all Sikhs have always treated every collection in the same way.

## 4. The Debunk

Use and identical status do not follow from each other. A community can recognise a prayer, study an associated text, honour a historical composition and still distinguish it from its Guru. Conversely, reporting a difference in practice is not an argument that the people practising it do not exist or are pretending.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Name the kind of authority being discussed: Guru, prescribed prayer, historical testimony, commentary or a particular community's practice. The question becomes clearer immediately. A claim about the central canon requires evidence about the canon, not simply a photograph of two volumes beside each other.

# Claim 03: Every divine name or mythological story is a command to worship its characters

The presence of a goddess, an avatar or a familiar mythological name proves that the writer instructed Sikhs to worship that figure as the supreme divine authority.

## 1. Where It Came From

A screenshot removes a name from its literary setting. The name is recognised, and recognition is treated as the end of interpretation. Yet a poet can invoke, adapt, criticise, recount or subordinate a figure. A name appearing in a sentence does not tell us which of those things the sentence is doing.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The opening of the Guru Granth Sahib places its affirmation in relation to the One. Its vocabulary also draws on different religious and linguistic worlds. The code's Article XVI makes a distinction between worship and studying other traditions. That distinction matters when approaching narratives associated with the tenth Guru: presence in literature is not identical to an instruction for worship. [SGGS1] [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

The court associated with Guru Gobind Singh was a literary as well as a political environment. Retelling inherited stories was a familiar activity in South Asian literary culture. The manuscript record and the particular composition matter more than a modern assumption that using an older story must mean accepting every theology previously attached to it. [MANN]

## 4. The Debunk

The blanket claim is invalid. To establish an instruction to worship, show the instruction, its speaker and its context. To establish an allegory, show the grounds for that reading too. Saying that every uncomfortable passage is automatically metaphor would be another unsupported shortcut. Interpretation requires more than either discomfort or enthusiasm.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Read a whole passage and compare plausible readings. Explain where the divine attributes are located and whether the narrative distinguishes a created figure from the ultimate reality. A responsible answer can acknowledge an interpretive dispute without declaring every use of a name either a betrayal or a victory.

# Claim 04: A character's behaviour is the author's moral instruction

A disturbing act described in a narrative proves that the author commands Sikhs to perform or approve that act.

## 1. Where It Came From

Charitropakhyan is often circulated through selected sexual or deceptive episodes. Some readers point to an episode as a direct command. Defenders sometimes answer that the entire work is only a harmless warning. Both answers can skip the difficult work: identifying narrators, audiences, plot and moral framing rather than reducing a narrative to a caption.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

A fictional or illustrative speaker is not automatically the normative voice of the Guru. Ethical comparison must identify the applicable teaching, not attach authority to everything a character says. The Sikh code sets expectations for sexual conduct; those are actual prescriptions, distinct from the behaviour depicted in a story. [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

Narratives can instruct through examples, counterexamples, irony and warning. Their production and reception also belong to a social world, with assumptions about gender and power that a present reader may contest. Authorship, literary purpose and contemporary ethical judgement remain separate questions. None disappears because a supporter says the passage must be good or a critic says it is bad.

## 4. The Debunk

Description is not automatically endorsement. That principle does not immunise literature from criticism. A work can depict harmful conduct and also convey a troubling view of women; a careful reader may argue that case from its pattern and framing. What fails is the assertion that the bare appearance of an act proves a direct religious order to repeat it.

## 5. The Honest Picture

State exactly what the text depicts and what interpretation you are making. Do not reproduce graphic extracts merely to shock. Do not tell an unsettled reader that concern proves disloyalty. Respectful study allows both literary context and moral questions, while refusing to turn an unexamined fragment into a verdict on a whole community.

# Claim 05: The Zafarnama is either an untouched original or worthless

Unless the surviving copy is the original sheet delivered to Aurangzeb, the Zafarnama can tell us nothing about Guru Gobind Singh or the conflict.

## 1. Where It Came From

The word original is used for several things: the earliest surviving manuscript, a composition in its original language, an autograph, and a modern edition derived from copies. Once those meanings are mixed, the absence of an autograph can be made to sound like the absence of any historical evidence at all.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The Zafarnama is a Persian work associated with the tenth Guru, not a section of the Guru Granth Sahib. Its ethical argument about broken oaths is relevant to Sikh history. That relevance does not make every modern translation a facsimile of a delivered letter, nor does it establish every story about the emperor's response. [SGPC-GG]

## 3. The Real History

Historical works often survive through copies. Researchers compare witnesses, language, provenance and references to events. Early Sikh sources and later collections provide a transmission history to examine. A modern rendering should disclose which edition it uses and whether line or couplet numbering follows that edition. [MANN] [SAINAPATI]

## 4. The Debunk

The alternative is false. A copied work can preserve historically valuable testimony; a cherished attribution can still require scrutiny. Neither fact proves that every word in every edition is identical. Most importantly, the letter's surviving argument does not establish that it converted Aurangzeb, killed him through remorse or produced an undocumented confession.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Use the work with an identified edition and an account of its transmission. Distinguish the letter's accusation from an independently confirmed detail, and both from a later story about its effect. You can take oath-breaking seriously without turning the person accused into a mind whose final thoughts the historian magically knows.

# Claim 06: The Sarbloh Granth is simply another name for the Guru Granth Sahib

The Sarbloh Granth, the Dasam Granth and the Guru Granth Sahib are interchangeable titles for one book with one history and one status.

## 1. Where It Came From

The word granth means a book or collection. Seeing it in several titles encourages a beginner to assume that all those titles identify the same object. Online arguments sometimes depend on that confusion: material from one work is presented as though it came from another, and a new reader has no obvious way to notice.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The Guru Granth Sahib has its own text and recognised canonical status. The Sarbloh Granth and Dasam Granth are distinct collections with their own reception and authorship questions. A verse attributed to the Sarbloh Granth cannot honestly be labelled Guru Granth Sahib, ang 1430, merely because both titles contain the word granth. [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

Different Sikh groups preserve and study different bodies of associated literature. To investigate the Sarbloh Granth, identify an actual manuscript or edition and the institution or scholar making an attribution. Do not infer its date from the date of the Guru Granth Sahib, or its reception everywhere from its reception in a particular tradition.

## 4. The Debunk

The titles are not interchangeable. This is a basic bibliographical correction, not a comprehensive verdict on the value of any of the works. Confusing the books destroys the possibility of checking the claim before the debate has even begun. A reader cannot compare evidence while the name of the evidence keeps changing.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Ask for four things: title, composition, location and edition. An ang citation belongs to the standardised Guru Granth Sahib system; another collection needs its own location. If the claimant cannot provide these, the quoted line may remain interesting, but its claimed source has not been established.

# Claim 07: A twentieth-century code is a word-for-word document from 1699

Every sentence in today's Sikh Rehat Maryada was recorded in the same form at the first Khalsa initiation and has never undergone collective discussion.

## 1. Where It Came From

A living discipline is traced to the founding Guru, and the trace is turned into a claim about the age of a particular English paragraph. Critics then point to modern compilation and say the whole discipline was recently invented. Both sides confuse the history of a tradition with the publication history of a particular code.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The code itself has a preface describing committees, meetings and drafts. Its rules are an institutional formulation of Sikh conduct, with authority claimed within that institutional setting. Its modern English wording is plainly not a recording made in an English-language meeting at Anandpur in 1699. [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

Earlier rahit literature is part of the evidence for developing discipline. Texts attributed to particular figures must be checked for dating and transmission rather than assigned automatically to the earliest possible year. A later code can consolidate inherited practice, settle some differences and leave others visible. Compilation does not mean creation from nothing. [MANN]

## 4. The Debunk

The claim of unchanged verbatim wording is wrong. The opposite claim that modern codification proves there was no earlier discipline is also wrong. We need continuity and change at the level of actual practices: which rule appears where, in which witness, and under whose authority? The committee's existence answers only part of that question.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Treat the current code as a current institutional source and earlier rahits as dated historical sources. A rule can be binding for a community without the historian pretending its exact printed sentence is three centuries old. Accurate dating makes the story of collective discipline more intelligible, not less serious.

# Claim 08: British involvement proves the Khalsa was a colonial invention

Because the British later favoured particular Sikh identities and practices in recruitment, they created the Khalsa and its visible discipline in the nineteenth century.

## 1. Where It Came From

Colonial officials classified communities and made selective forms of identity useful for recruitment. That real intervention is enlarged into a claim that the community did not previously exist. The chronology disappears: a later state influencing an institution is treated as though it had invented the institution's entire earlier history.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Early works associated with the Guru's period and its aftermath discuss the Khalsa before British annexation of Punjab in 1849. Sainapati's Sri Gur Sobha is especially relevant, while its own date, literary purpose and manuscript witnesses must still be evaluated. These sources cannot all be dated by the later recruitment policy. [SAINAPATI] [MANN]

## 3. The Real History

British recruitment and racial classification affected who was favoured, how communities represented themselves and which traits officials praised. Streets studies this imperial construction of martial identities. An effect on a pre-existing community is a historically meaningful claim. It is different from inventing that community retrospectively. [STREETS]

## 4. The Debunk

The chronology defeats the blanket invention claim. It does not defeat evidence of colonial influence. We should reject both the erasure of precolonial Sikh history and the fantasy that colonial rule left Sikh institutions untouched. A single sentence about British preferences cannot do the work of tracing either continuity or reform.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Ask what existed before colonial involvement and what changed during it. Make the comparison using dated evidence. This method lets the reader recognise Sikh agency alongside imperial pressure, rather than reducing Sikhs either to puppets of a government or to people magically insulated from the society in which they lived.

# Claim 09: Old paper proves old authorship

A manuscript on old-looking paper, photographed beside an impressive caption, establishes that every line was written by Guru Gobind Singh himself.

## 1. Where It Came From

Material age has a powerful visual effect. Brown paper, a handwritten script and a library shelf can feel more convincing than a careful argument. The caption then supplies the missing author and date. But the age of a support, the date of copying, the age of a composition and the identity of its composer are different measurements.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Respect for scripture does not require pretending that a copyist is the original composer. A copied shabad can faithfully transmit a Guru's composition without being written in the Guru's hand. Similarly, a collection can contain texts of different origins. The relevant religious question and the relevant material question need their own evidence.

## 3. The Real History

Researchers inspect colophons, handwriting, paper, inks, ownership marks, internal dates and comparison with other witnesses. A colophon is a note about production or completion; it too can be copied. Provenance is the traceable history of an object's custody. Neither technical testing nor visual inspection alone answers every question about a manuscript. [MANN]

## 4. The Debunk

Old appearance does not prove an autograph. It also does not prove a forgery. A genuine old copy and an unsupported authorship caption can coexist. Request the catalogue record, the relevant folio and the argument for attribution. If access is restricted, say that direct verification was unavailable rather than inventing a result.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Match confidence to the evidence actually inspected. Photographing a manuscript is the beginning of documentation, not its conclusion. The most useful answer may be that a work is witnessed by a certain date while the identity of its original composer remains disputed. That is a precise result, even when it is less dramatic.

# Claim 10: A reader's loyalty settles a textual disagreement

Anyone who asks about authorship is attacking the tenth Guru, and anyone who accepts an associated composition is incapable of critical thought.

## 1. Where It Came From

Arguments about sacred books are also arguments about belonging. Once that emotional pressure enters, a question can be treated as a confession, and a religious commitment as evidence of stupidity. The actual manuscript problem becomes secondary. People are pushed to defend their identity before they can inspect the evidence.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Truthful living in Siri Raag, ang 62, gives no reason to call an unsupported statement true simply because it comforts the group. At the same time, a text's religious significance cannot be assessed merely by mocking its readers. The commitment being discussed must be described honestly, whether the writer shares it or does not. [SGGS62]

## 3. The Real History

Textual disagreements occur within traditions as well as between them. Institutions, scholars and practising communities can differ about attribution, reading and ritual use. Such disagreement needs dated and named accounts. Calling one side an infiltrator or the other side brainwashed explains nothing about the origin of a manuscript.

## 4. The Debunk

Neither loyalty accusation is an authorship argument. Evidence does not become stronger when the person asking for it is insulted. A careful critical answer can reject a claim while preserving the distinction between the claim, the claimant and the entire community. That distinction is a requirement of accuracy as much as of decency.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Make room for precise disagreement. Present the best evidence for the interpretation you accept and the serious objections to it. Do not promise that every issue is settled, and do not use uncertainty as permission to say anything. A tradition deserves readers capable of a patient answer more than it needs another forced loyalty test.

# Closing

The central result is a set of distinctions, not a secret answer about a whole collection. Authorship is not the same as compilation. Compilation is not the same as canon. Canon is not the same as every liturgical use. A narrator is not always an author, and a depiction is not automatically an instruction. A copied witness is not an autograph; it is not therefore worthless.

The easy argument asks for one grand yes or no. The useful argument asks what the claim refers to, how early the relevant evidence is, what the text actually says and what remains unsettled. This is slower, but it avoids declaring a whole community's intelligence or loyalty on the strength of a fragment.

The earlier writing is background, not another independent early witness.
