# Foreword

Equality is easiest to praise when nobody asks who is allowed to marry whom, control a committee, inherit property or report abuse. A community can sincerely teach equal human worth and still reproduce unequal treatment. Pointing to that gap is not proof that the teaching is worthless. Refusing to see it is not proof that the teaching has succeeded.

This chapter keeps three kinds of statement apart. Gurbani supplies religious and ethical teaching. A conduct code states an institution's prescriptions. Historical and social evidence describes what people have done. None of those sources can silently replace the other two. A rule against caste prejudice does not establish that no Sikh practises it. An instance of prejudice does not establish that the Guru instructed it.

I also want the person at the receiving end of a rule to remain visible. It is not enough to say that everyone sits together for one meal if someone is treated as unacceptable for marriage or leadership afterwards. Nor is it enough to call a restriction tradition without identifying its source. Equality becomes a serious claim when it is examined in the places where comfort, status and power make it difficult.

# Claim 01: The Gurus' teaching made caste discrimination disappear

Because Sikh teaching rejects hierarchy by birth, caste discrimination cannot exist among Sikhs.

## 1. Where It Came From

A principle is presented as a completed social result. The move is comforting: the community's teaching is admirable, so nobody within it needs to examine their conduct. A report of discrimination can then be dismissed as an attack on the teaching, even when the reporter is appealing to that very teaching for protection.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The scripture's inclusion of Bhagat Ravidas and other voices challenges the idea that a birth label determines whose teaching deserves reverence. Article XVI of the code rejects caste and untouchability; Article XVIII tells Sikh couples not to choose by caste and descent. These are normative commitments, not results of a social survey. [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

Kiyotaka Sato's field research on four Leicester gurdwaras examines caste-related divisions through interviews and institutional history. It is evidence for a particular setting and period, not a census of every Sikh worldwide. That limited evidence is enough to defeat the absolute statement that caste discrimination cannot occur within Sikh communities. [CASTE-RESEARCH]

## 4. The Debunk

The inference is false. Teaching against a practice and eliminating the practice are different achievements. Equally, evidence of discrimination does not prove that all Sikhs discriminate or that every institution is identical. The responsible conclusion names the gap and the relevant evidence without replacing one universal stereotype with another.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Take the prohibition seriously by asking where behaviour contradicts it. Examine marriage preferences, access to committees, treatment of workers and the experience of people reporting exclusion. A community's principle becomes a resource for correcting discrimination when it is allowed to challenge the community, rather than being used to deny the discrimination in advance.

# Claim 02: A caste-associated surname proves every belief and action of its bearer

Anyone using a surname associated with a caste necessarily believes in birth-based superiority and can be judged without any other information.

## 1. Where It Came From

Names carry social information, so the information is treated as a complete moral biography. That lets an observer classify a stranger quickly. It also allows someone to declare themselves beyond prejudice merely by changing a name, while continuing to sort prospective spouses and neighbours by the same old hierarchy.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The code's rules about caste concern belief and conduct, including marriage. They cannot be fulfilled only through a name on a document. Singh and Kaur have strong significance in Sikh identity, but the appearance of a shared name does not establish the disappearance of the person's preferences or the surrounding society's classifications. [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

Family and caste-associated labels can remain in social, administrative and migration contexts. Their use may signal pride, habit, kinship, external classification or discrimination; the actual setting matters. A researcher needs more than a surname to establish what a particular person believes, and more than a sample of names to measure discriminatory treatment.

## 4. The Debunk

The automatic judgement is unjustified. It does not follow that names are socially irrelevant. They can help reproduce a hierarchy or expose someone to it, and a claim of harmless habit should be examined when exclusion follows. The error is treating a clue as a complete finding without investigating the action under discussion.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Ask how a person treats other people and what their naming choices do in that context. A name alone is insufficient for a verdict about character. So is a public denial of caste. The stronger evidence is what happens when an actual marriage, job, invitation or leadership role crosses a socially maintained boundary.

# Claim 03: Every separate gurdwara proves either harmless diversity or universal hatred

A gurdwara associated with a particular community must be either completely unrelated to caste or proof that every other Sikh excludes that community.

## 1. Where It Came From

An institution's existence is used as a shortcut around its history. Defenders may call every division a preference; critics may describe every division as the same act of exclusion. Both positions remove the people who built the institution and the reasons they gave for doing so.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Equal regard and the rejection of untouchability set a standard against which an institution can be assessed. Kanara, ang 1299, refuses to treat another person as an enemy or stranger. That teaching does not tell us the origin of every building. The origin must be established through that building's actual history and participants. [SGGS1299]

## 3. The Real History

Sato's Leicester study describes different trajectories, including community organisation and reported discrimination. Institutional separation can also involve language, occupational networks, locality, management disputes and religious identification. Causes can combine. An account should not erase reported exclusion by selecting a harmless possible cause that has not been shown to explain the case. [CASTE-RESEARCH]

## 4. The Debunk

Neither universal inference works. One institution can document a serious local failure without establishing a belief held by all Sikhs. A separate institution can provide solidarity without making the circumstances that prompted it harmless. Ask about access, leadership, founding decisions and testimony rather than resolving the question through the name on the gate.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Let participants explain the history, then compare their accounts with records and other testimony. A fair inquiry includes people with less institutional power. It distinguishes what an organisation does now from why it was founded, and it does not treat the absence of a complaint at a public meeting as proof of equal treatment.

# Claim 04: Every Ravidassia has one identical religious identity

All Ravidassia people identify themselves in exactly the same way, so outsiders may decide their religion for them from a caste label.

## 1. Where It Came From

A community name is read as if it were a permanent box with one official answer. That simplifies a complex history of devotion, caste discrimination, reform and institutional change. It can also become a fight over ownership: a group's experience is admitted only if its identity fits the category another community prefers.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Bhagat Ravidas's compositions belong to the Guru Granth Sahib. That canonical fact matters. It does not determine every living follower's self-identification, every institution's scripture or every later movement's relationship to Sikh institutions. Revering a contributor and classifying all people associated with that contributor are separate acts.

## 3. The Real History

Field research documents changing and overlapping identities in particular settings. Sato distinguishes caste-group descriptions from religious identification and traces institutions through people's accounts. Historical labels should be reported in their period, with present self-descriptions checked separately. A 2012 article is not a current membership register for every Ravidassia institution in 2026. [CASTE-RESEARCH]

## 4. The Debunk

The claim of one compulsory identity overreaches. A caste label does not settle an individual's religious commitment. Nor should a writer take one organisation's announcement and assign it to everyone sharing an ancestry. A responsible account names the particular person, institution or movement and allows differences within the broader community.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Use people's own stated identification wherever it is relevant and available. Explain how identities changed without treating change as deceit. The aim is to understand a community's history and concerns, including exclusion, rather than to make its members settle someone else's argument about who may claim them.

# Claim 05: Sikh identity makes Scheduled Caste recognition legally impossible

No Sikh can be recognised as Scheduled Caste in India because Sikh doctrine rejects caste.

## 1. Where It Came From

A religious principle is substituted for a statutory definition. The claim often appears as a supposed contradiction: if someone is Sikh and uses a legal protection addressing caste disadvantage, they must admit the religion endorses caste. That confuses a protection responding to social conditions with a religious approval of those conditions.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Rejecting caste hierarchy is a religious commitment. It does not itself amend the Constitution or a statutory order. The teaching can help explain why discrimination is wrong while the legal system uses specified categories to respond to its effects. Those purposes are not logically identical and do not have to cancel each other.

## 3. The Real History

Paragraph 3 of the Constitution (Scheduled Castes) Order, as amended, includes Hindu, Sikh and Buddhist professions of religion within its religious condition. The 1956 amendment is relevant to Sikh inclusion. Recognition also depends on the applicable notified community and territorial and other legal requirements. This is not a finding that every Sikh qualifies. [SC-ORDER] [SC-1956]

## 4. The Debunk

The universal impossibility claim is false under the cited statutory framework. Individual eligibility cannot be decided from a religious label alone, and this chapter is not an eligibility certificate. The legal recognition of disadvantage also does not prove that the Guru approved the hierarchy producing it.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Read the actual order and the relevant jurisdiction's requirements when a legal decision is needed. For the religious discussion, address the injustice and the community's response. The question is whether people face disadvantage and how it is remedied, not whether a declaration of equality should make their experience legally invisible.

# Claim 06: Women are barred by Sikh teaching from reading or leading worship

The Sikh conduct code permits men alone to attend on the Guru Granth Sahib, read for the congregation, conduct a marriage ceremony or administer initiation.

## 1. Where It Came From

An observed custom is called the religious rule. If people usually see men performing a role, they may infer that women are prohibited. A local restriction can then acquire the authority of scripture without anyone producing a passage. The familiarity of the arrangement does the work of evidence.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Guru Nanak's Asa salok at ang 473 challenges contempt for women. The code expressly names women in attendance on the Guru Granth Sahib, allows a male or female marriage officiant and includes women among the six initiated Sikhs serving at an initiation. These permissions refute a blanket male-only claim. [SGGS473] [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

A formal permission does not prove equal participation at every shrine or in every committee. Barriers can operate through recruitment, training, family expectations, appointment practices or local custom. A claim about a particular institution needs its current rules and observed practice; neither one automatically describes all institutions.

## 4. The Debunk

The blanket prohibition is false under the cited code. The blanket claim that every role is equally accessible everywhere would also be unsupported. The useful conclusion separates eligibility on paper, actual participation and institutional authority. A photograph of a male group establishes who appears in that photograph, not a universal religious prohibition.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Ask for the stated basis of any restriction and compare it with the relevant provision. Encourage training and meaningful access rather than treating one symbolic appointment as a complete solution. If an institution follows a different discipline, describe that discipline accurately and allow its reasoning to be questioned.

# Claim 07: Menstruation makes a woman spiritually unfit to enter a gurdwara

Gurbani establishes a universal menstrual ban on Sikh worship, so excluding a menstruating woman requires no further justification.

## 1. Where It Came From

Ideas about bodily impurity circulate through families and surrounding cultures. A prohibition may be passed down as something everyone knows, without a scriptural location. A physiological process is then made into a judgement about a person's access to the divine. The people affected may be discouraged from asking for the source.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The Asa saloks at angs 472–473 examine purity, falsehood, birth-related pollution and contempt for women. They relocate the ethical problem toward the mind and conduct. Read the connected passages rather than using a bodily comparison at ang 472 as a stand-alone exclusion rule. The code's worship provisions do not state the claimed universal menstrual ban. [SGGS472] [SGGS473] [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

A local practice can exist without being prescribed by the Guru Granth Sahib. To establish its history, identify who applies it, when it appeared and what authority is claimed. The existence of similar practices elsewhere is evidence of a wider social setting, not proof that Sikh scripture commands the practice.

## 4. The Debunk

The asserted universal scriptural ban has not been established and contradicts the direction of the cited purity critique. This does not mean a person must disregard comfort or health. Voluntarily resting because of symptoms is different from being declared spiritually contaminating. Do not disguise exclusion as care when the person's own choice is removed.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Allow participation and ordinary personal choice without stigma. When a restriction is asserted, request the complete text and the institutional rule. A useful explanation identifies actual hygiene needs without making menstruation evidence of moral or spiritual inferiority. The body's ordinary functions are not a warrant to silence the person living in it.

# Claim 08: The name Kaur proves complete social equality

Because Sikh women share the name Kaur, dowry pressure, unequal power and restrictions on women's choices cannot exist within Sikh families.

## 1. Where It Came From

A powerful identity is asked to prove a complete social outcome. It is the same mistake made with Singh and caste. The symbol becomes a reason to stop looking. If a woman reports an unequal relationship, the report is treated as an insult to a name intended to express dignity.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Ang 473 challenges denigration of women; the code includes provisions against practices such as female infanticide and prescribes responsibilities in marriage. These commitments provide standards for conduct. They cannot honestly be presented as a statistical finding that every family already satisfies those standards. [SGGS473] [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

Names interact with inheritance, labour, family authority and institutions. A woman's ability to study, control resources, refuse a marriage or report harm needs examination in its own setting. Describing such questions does not require inventing an all-Sikh prevalence figure. It requires refusing to let a symbol answer a question about actual power.

## 4. The Debunk

The inference fails. A shared name does not certify equal treatment. Nor does evidence of unequal treatment make the name meaningless. A symbol can supply a language for challenging a failure. The decisive question is whether people are allowed to use that language when the challenge concerns their own household.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Respect the name by taking the person seriously. Examine consent, opportunity, economic control and safety. Equality is more than praising a daughter while denying her a voice in decisions about her life. A community's declared dignity becomes credible when it protects the right to raise that uncomfortable concern.

# Claim 09: Religious marriage rules and civil marriage rights are the same thing

If an institution restricts Anand Karaj, it establishes that the people concerned have no possible civil marriage rights, and equal respect means every ceremony has no religious conditions.

## 1. Where It Came From

Two disputes are collapsed: who may participate in a religious rite, and what rights people have under civil law. One side treats a religious condition as total control over a person's life. Another treats equal human dignity as proof that no religious ceremony can have a defined theological purpose.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

Article XVIII of the code sets conditions for Anand Karaj. These are prescriptions for a Sikh religious ceremony. They should be read and discussed accurately, including their wording about Sikh marriage. A general affirmation of human worth does not make those printed provisions disappear, and the provisions do not themselves determine every civil-law option. [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

The Anand Marriage Act is a civil statute dealing with marriages solemnised through the Anand ceremony. India also has a separate civil-marriage framework under the Special Marriage Act. The applicable legal requirements and current interpretation need qualified advice in a particular case. A gurdwara committee's view is not a substitute for reading the law. [ANAND-ACT] [SMA]

## 4. The Debunk

The categories are different. Stating that distinction is not a ruling on a particular couple's eligibility or a declaration that every institution's policy is identical. It also does not justify intimidation. People can debate a religious ceremony's conditions while respecting the safety, consent and legal rights of those involved.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Describe the religious rule, the institution's current practice and the legal question separately. A couple deserves clear information before arrangements are made. Neither an invented promise nor a public threat makes the situation easier. Do not make people discover a contested condition only when they are already vulnerable to disruption.

# Claim 10: Equal human worth means every institutional rule is already beyond criticism

Because Sikh teaching affirms dignity, the current conduct code and every institutional practice contain no restriction that a modern reader could reasonably question.

## 1. Where It Came From

An admirable principle becomes a shield around every document associated with it. Selective quotation makes that shield seem stronger. A writer highlights inclusive provisions and omits an uncomfortable clause, then claims the text has no tension. That is advocacy through incomplete reporting rather than careful reading.

## 2. What the Sikh Texts Say

The code's Article XXIV includes women and allows initiation across backgrounds, but its subsection on the five administering initiation also contains exclusions concerning disability and chronic disease. A complete account must acknowledge that wording. It is a rule in an institutional code, not evidence that disabled people have lesser human worth. [SRM]

## 3. The Real History

Institutions make rules within particular circumstances and inherited assumptions. Readers can ask what a restriction was intended to do, whether it has a defensible basis and how it affects people now. Formal authority and ethical criticism are different questions. Calling a document authoritative within an institution does not settle every critique of its content.

## 4. The Debunk

The claim of an entirely criticism-proof institution is unsupported. This chapter cannot praise inclusive wording while concealing restrictive wording on the same page. Nor should it turn one clause into a claim that all Sikhs despise disabled people. Precision requires identifying the clause and the question it raises.

## 5. The Honest Picture

Make the principle available for institutional examination, including accessibility and reasonable participation. A fair reader can value a code, describe its authority and still ask whether a provision deserves reconsideration. Honest respect requires the whole relevant page, especially when the omitted sentence affects someone with less power to insist on being heard.

# Closing

The teaching, the rule and the result are three different things. Keeping them apart stops equality from becoming a claim that nobody is allowed to test. It also stops a community's failures from being used as an excuse to deny the value of its best commitments.

This part has deliberately included an uncomfortable provision in the conduct code alongside its inclusive ones. The method would mean little if I selected only the wording that made my argument easy. The same responsibility applies to names, shared meals, caste divisions and marriage: identify the actual statement and ask what happens to actual people.
